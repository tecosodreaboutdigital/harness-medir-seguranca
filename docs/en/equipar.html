Step 2 of 5 · E

Equip

What does the model need to know and be able to do? The minimum to help a tired operator decide, and nothing more.

What it is for

Equipping means writing the harness: role, protocol, control level, answer format, and what the model does not know. The model helps the operator decide. It does not decide.

The sizing rule matters more here than anywhere else: the harness must be smaller than the failure surface it controls. Every extra tool is one more way to fail, and at 3 a.m. nobody has time to find out which.

Questions

  1. What is the model's role in this control room? (helping the operator decide, never deciding)
  2. Which part of the control room protocol applies to this event?
  3. What is the control level, and who decides at each step?
  4. What format does the operator need, knowing they are tired, alone and have other alerts waiting?
  5. Which tools does the model really need? Query recorded video? Open a ticket? Anything else?
  6. What does the model not know, and needs to say it does not know? (camera condition, who is on duty, whether the area was cleared)
  7. Where do decisions live once the conversation is over?

What comes out

  • The prompt with harness for this task, versioned (see prompt with harness).
  • A closed list of tools, each with what it does and how it fails.
  • The answer format, with a length limit.
  • Where every alert, decision and action is logged (see the minimum record).

In CCTV

  • Short, fixed answer. Most likely hypothesis and two alternatives, one of them a false positive; what to check in the next 60 seconds; who decides and when to escalate; what to log; what it does not know. 120 words at most.
  • Minimal tools at remote energy sites. The model may pull the last few minutes of recorded video and open a ticket linked to the alert. It may not release the digital key to the equipment cabinet.
  • Minimal tools in a residential building. The model may bring the camera up on the video wall and draft the message to the front desk. The operator is the one who talks to the front desk.
  • Shift context. Telling the model how many alerts the operator received in the last hour changes the answer: with 38 alerts in the queue, the recommendation has to be shorter and more selective.
Integrations

Connecting the model to the video management system, access control or infrastructure monitoring always starts read-only. The integracao-vms, integracao-controle-de-acesso and saude-da-operacao skills show the pattern.

Anti-patterns

  • A prompt with no role. "What should I do?" gets a generic, long, overconfident answer. Compare in the A versus B exercise.
  • Too many tools. Giving the model access control, watch lists and people records "because it might be useful" widens the failure surface and raises the control level.
  • A harness that lives in someone's head. A rule that is not written in the prompt, the protocol or the configuration is not a rule.
  • Forgetting what the model does not know. A model that never says "I don't know" makes things up with the same confidence it uses when it is right.
  • Real data in a public assistant. Test prompts with fictitious alerts. Real faces, plates and names do not go into general-purpose assistants; personal data handling follows the applicable privacy law and your company's policy.
